# Antikythera mechanism: exhibit notes

The Antikythera mechanism was found in 1901 by sponge divers in a shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera. It is a bronze geared device, now in 82 fragments at the National Archaeological Museum in Athens, dated to roughly 150 to 100 BC.

## What it did

It is an astronomical calculator. The front dial shows the position of the sun and moon in the zodiac and a lunar phase ball. The back has two spiral dials: the Metonic dial (235 months, the 19-year calendar cycle) and the Saros dial (223 months), which predicts solar and lunar eclipses. A small Exeligmos dial corrects the eclipse times.

## The games dial

A subsidiary dial on the back tracks the four-year cycle of the Panhellenic games: Olympia, Nemea, Isthmia and Delphi (the Pythian games), with Naa and Halieia on the inner ring. It was identified in 2008 from the CT scans. For the exhibit, this is the dial the second room is built around: the device was also a calendar of civic life, not only an instrument of the sky.

## Gearing

At least 30 surviving gears, with teeth about 1.5 mm. Nothing of comparable complexity is known until the astronomical clocks of 14th-century Europe. The pin-and-slot mechanism models the moon's varying speed, which is the piece the gearing room should explain with a working model.
